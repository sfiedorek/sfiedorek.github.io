On Hobbies

It seems like everyone's going through a hobby renaissance right now. All my friends are learning how to knit, joining a local sports team, or buying board games. Just in the past few months, I’ve started cross stitching, I bought air-dry clay to make decor for my room, and I’m embarrassed to admit that I’ve looked up “DJing for beginners.” While I don’t want to project, I suspect that people are turning to hobbies as a way to reject the constant demand for productivity that centuries of American Protestant work ethic has led us to. Hobbies are inherently anti-production, even where they result in a product. When I knit a sweater, it takes me about six months, and I spend more on the yarn than it would cost to just buy the sweater. It’s just for fun, and it’s just for me. When everything else in life feels corporately mandated, the joy of a hobby is a necessity.

More practically, I think everyone wants a hobby because we’re sick of being on our phone. These days, it feels like most people, especially those my age, kind of hate the Internet. In 2022, the New York Times wrote a profile on a group of Brooklyn teenagers calling themselves the Luddite Club1. The kids had traded in iPhones for flip phones and met once a week to read, sketch, or sew—any hobbies were acceptable as long as no screens were involved. Their vision spread quickly. Today, the Luddite Club has a simple website that lists chapters in 21 states and D.C.2

But while some people are choosing to log off as much as possible, others are attempting to reshape the Internet. The “small web” or “indie web” is a growing number of sites that revive the charm of building and visiting personal websites. The small web has personality that the Big Tech Internet lacks. There are no third-party cookies following your every move, no annoying pop-up ads that slow down the loading, no incessant reminders to subscribe for more features.3 Indie web designers aren’t trying to make money from your data, they’re just enjoying messing with HTML/CSS and sharing their work with the world. Many people also view the small web as an activist project, taking our ownership of the web back from the corporate tech world that has abused it.4 For others (though the two are not mutually exclusive), the indie web is just a hobby.

Stamp collecting is a rare example of an old hobby that has always been just for fun. While yes, there are people making money off the hobby, the vast majority of collectors are in it for pure love of the game. So many modern pastimes have their origins in labor. Knitting was once required of women; it was how clothes got made. The Internet seems to have gone in the opposite direction. The World Wide Web of the 90s and early 2000s was dominated by personal webpages, hosted on GeoCities and the like. I’d like to refer to these sites as hobby sites, since their primary purpose was just the designer’s entertainment. Many of these were themselves dedicated to the designer’s real life hobbies5. Over time however, the authorship has been stripped from the Internet, taking away exactly the aspects that hobby site builders enjoyed. At this moment, when everyone is seeking offline hobbies, I think a more radical move is to make the Internet a hobby again. Let’s build and find spaces populated by real people, with real artistic visions.

And so, I propose...

A Call to Action

  1. Start collecting stamps.
  2. Make the Internet a hobby again.
  3. Reclaim the Web from technocrats.

Should be easy, right?
