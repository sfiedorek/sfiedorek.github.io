History of Stamp Collecting

The United Kingdom released the first modern, adhesive poster stamp, called the "Penny Black" in 1840. Countries around the world quickly followed suit, and adhesive postage stamps quickly became standard for sending mail. Even as stamps were brand new, individuals started collecting. By the late nineteenth century, collecting practices were standardizing, and the hobby was already becoming popular. The word 'philately,' functionally synonymous with 'stamp collecting,' was coined in 1865, according to the Oxford English Dictionary.1

As such a popular hobby, a number of famous twentieth century figures enjoyed stamp collecting, from Franklin Roosevelt and King George V to John Lennon.

Today, philatelists can build a collection at expos, professional auctions, bourse halls hosted by a local club, or online via sites like Ebay. Collectors may specialize in collecting stamps from a particular country or region. Others pursue topical stamp collecting, a collection based on any theme, like sailboats, animals, or a holiday.

Identifying Stamps

When looking at lots of stamps from around the world, it can be hard to know right away what you're looking at. To get very specific, you can consult a stamp catalog (available for most major countries), but it can even been tricky to know what country the stamp is from! Some places use unfamiliar markers or inscriptions to identify their postage. Recognizing these is very important. Here are a few that I've come across:

Inscription Country
Eesti Estonia
Dienstmarke Germany (literally "official stamp")
Helvetia Switzerland
Magyar Posta Hungary
Montevideo Uruguay
No inscription! Because they invented postage stamps, the United Kingdom is the only country that isn't required to put their name on their stamps.

Collectors on the Web

Stamp collecting is often seen as an old man’s hobby, and, well, that’s not fully wrong. Young stamp collectors are pretty rare, and the philatelic world remains largely confined to in-person meetups hosted by long-standing societies. However, the stamp world IS online. Most philatelic clubs run a website, which posts their event schedules, publications produced by the club, and information for collectors. There are also a host of web-based resources for both new and old collectors. Some of my favorite websites include:

  • American Philatelic Society, the national stamp club based in Bellefonte, PA; the APS also manages a huge research library, much of which can be accessed online
  • Collectors Club, the homepage for NYC's largest stamp club, founded in 1896
  • Stamp Smarter, a personal website which hosts dozens of articles on stamp collecting (and is adorably dedicated to the creator's wife)
  • Smithsonian National Postal Museum, whose digital collections cover all of US postal history and who share additional info for today's collectors
  • And of course, the USPS online store to buy all the new stamp releases!
